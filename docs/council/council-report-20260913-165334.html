Is Headroom publishable, after seven retractions?

Five advisors on four models, five independent reviewers, one chairman's verdict. Graded against the bar Raymond chose: publishable as undergraduate research.

2026-09-13 · 16:53:34 · upgraded panel · split decision (C×2, B×2, D×1)
B+
Advisors offered C+ (Contrarian) and B+/A− (Executor). The chairman lands nearer the Executor, but only because two reviewers independently broke the Contrarian's central argument.

The measurements are not in question and never have been. What is in question is one sentence: how far the causal result generalises.

Seven retractions sound like a project failing. Read the list and they are all the same failure repeated — a claim about what was new, never a claim about what was measured. No CSV has ever been wrong. That distinction is the difference between a B+ and the C+ the Contrarian wanted to give, and it is why this is publishable work that is not yet ready to send.

The honest statement of the headline result is "strong within-chip causal evidence, generalisation uncertain" — not "proven", and not the Contrarian's "pseudoreplicated, period."

Where the council agrees

All five, including the harshest, accept that the experimental work is sound: registered predictions before collection, a real negative control, a boundary condition reported rather than buried, and a null published as a headline instead of tuned away. Nobody disputed a number.

All five also agree the framing is the liability, not the data — and that the contribution sentence's opening clause invites a reviewer to read the whole causal design as ordinary undervolting and stop reading.

Where it clashes

Clash 1 — is the causal result pseudoreplicated?

The Contrarian says the centrepiece is fatally weak: "+465 MHz in 12 of 12 workloads" is one chip, twelve correlated measurements, one curve edit — "four chips across three architectures sounds like breadth; it is four n=1 studies stacked." The Executor and First Principles treat the same evidence as the paper's spine.

Adjudicated, and the Contrarian loses half of it. Reviewers 2 and 4, working independently, found the same flaw: the Contrarian calls the negative control weak because it shares chip, session and operator — which is precisely what a negative control is for. That is confusing narrow scope (true) with invalid design (false).

What survives from it is the more valuable half: generalisation across chips is genuinely unsupported, and a DVFS reviewer will say so within thirty seconds.

Clash 2 — ship, stop, or reframe?

The Outsider says stop and do a real literature review first — "they're doing literature search backwards." The Executor says ship: four items, then submit. First Principles says the question is wrong, and the falsification loop is itself the paper.

The chairman sides with the Executor on sequencing and the Outsider on cause — the search discipline does need rebuilding, but a 2–4 week reading pause inside a closing window is how a project dies with nothing submitted.

Clash 3 — is the audit infrastructure rigour or avoidance?

The Contrarian: the 282 claims and 701 tests "are not rigour — they are displacement," effort poured into the one axis that has never been the failure mode. The Expansionist: it is the single most transferable thing in the project and a paper in its own right.

Both are right and they are not in conflict. The auditor has never caught a novelty error because it cannot — but it has caught four wrong numbers, and internal consistency not being this project's failure mode is not an argument that it is worthless.

Blind spots the review caught

Reviewer 1 — the sharpest catch in the session

Nobody proposed external verification of novelty. The project has a documented 0-for-7 record at judging what the literature already contains — yet every single recommendation assumes Raymond will now do that correctly, alone. "D's reframing is especially dangerous because it lowers the novelty bar, potentially creating false confidence in searches he has already failed at."

The mentorship exists to support publication and is the obvious instrument for exactly this. It is not being used that way.

Reviewer 5 — the most practically urgent

Nobody knows the actual deadline. Every recommendation in the packet scales to infinite time. "The mentorship 'gets the work out', which probably means delivering a credible artifact before a hard date, not achieving journal acceptance." Which of these plans is even reachable depends entirely on a date nobody stated.

Reviewer 2 — the one that would improve the science

Nobody asked what the 2060 Super failure means. All five treat it as a scope limitation. It is evidence about which hardware properties determine whether the rule applies at all — does it require sub-6 mV stepping? That is a mechanism question, and it is where the understanding is.

Reviewer 3 — on the person, not the artifact

First Principles ignores asymmetric career risk: an eighth retraction caught after publication reads very differently to a hiring committee than one caught in a private repo. "D's philosophical reframe is elegant but doesn't say what to do tomorrow."

Reviewer 4 — the contrarian proposal nobody else made

The null result could be the first publication: a genuine negative-result contribution, reachable in weeks, establishing a record and buying time for the harder claims. The Contrarian called it "publishable nowhere"; three advisors ignored it entirely.

The recommendation

Do not reframe the project as a methods paper about its own falsification loop. It is the most intellectually attractive option on the table and the chairman is the least qualified voice to endorse it — see the disclosure below. It also fails Reviewer 3's test: it does not tell you what to do tomorrow, and it trades a concrete empirical result for a meta-claim that a reviewer outside this repo has no reason to find remarkable.

Instead, keep the causal result as the headline and fix the one thing that makes it breakable. The Contrarian's fix is the best single action anyone in the session proposed, and it was on nobody's to-do list:

  1. A second RTX 5060 Ti. Repeat the A/B/B/A manipulation on a different physical chip of the same model. This converts the weakest point — one chip — into a replication, and Raymond can reach it because he sells these. No other researcher in this literature has that asset cheaply.
  2. The 2060 Super sweep rides along in the same elevated session. Ten minutes, already queued, and it is the first measured Turing V/F curve in this literature.
  3. Re-weight the contribution sentence while the sweeps run. The technique is standard; the causal test is not. Make the sentence say so.
  4. Ask the mentor to verify novelty before anything is submitted — the one control this project has never applied to the one axis where it is 0-for-7.
  5. Get the deadline in writing and cut the plan to fit it.

Park the dataset-publication prep and the ML thread. Both are real; neither blocks submission; both will expand to fill whatever time remains.

The one thing to do first

Put a second RTX 5060 Ti on the bench and re-run the floor manipulation on it.

Everything else in this report is an improvement to a paper. This is the difference between a result a reviewer can dismiss in one sentence and one they cannot. It uses the single advantage this project has over better-funded labs — physical access to multiple copies of the same consumer chip — and it is the only item that turns n=1 into replication.

If a second card cannot be reached in the window, then the Contrarian is right about the fallback: state the limitation plainly in the abstract, and target a workshop or artifact track rather than a venue whose reviewers will treat it as fatal.

The advisors, in full

Response B — "the survivors are selected by the same process that produced seven retractions"Contrarian · Opus

Every surviving claim is phrased as not found elsewhere. This project has a documented 0-for-7 record at predicting what the literature already contains, and each retraction came from searching after writing. The process hasn't changed; only the vocabulary has. Apply that base rate to the three survivors and you should expect one or two to fall — most likely the crossbar chain, given LACT documented the domain a fortnight before you measured it.

The statistical flaw nobody has named: the causal result is pseudoreplicated. "+465 MHz in 12 of 12 workloads" is n=1 chip, 12 correlated measurements on one piece of silicon with one curve edit. Twelve workloads on one GB206 is not twelve trials. Four chips across three architectures sounds like breadth; it is four n=1 studies stacked.

And the manipulation is hand-made in Afterburner. "Openly and reproducibly" is half a claim: the data is open, the intervention is a GUI drag nobody can replicate exactly.

The 282 claims, 701 tests and CI are not rigour — they are displacement.

Grade: C+. Excellent lab notebook, a paper with no defensible novelty claim left. Kill: the Ridge model thread, further chips, further auditor coverage, and the word "first" in any form. Next: replicate the A/B/B/A manipulation on a second 5060 Ti — you sell these. That single run is worth more than everything else queued.

Response C — "Monday morning, in order"Executor · Sonnet

1. Run the 2060 Super sweep first — before coffee gets cold. ~10 minutes, and the cheapest thing on the list relative to what it buys: a boundary condition that is currently ±60 MHz goes to ±10, and it is the first measured Turing V/F curve in this literature.

2. Same GPU session: build the two floor-ladder profiles and run their suites. He's already elevated, already at the machine. Batching is the only way it happens this month. This converts "the optimum moves" into "the optimum moves proportionally" — the difference between a demo and a dose-response curve. That's the paper's spine.

3. While sweeps run — rewrite the contribution sentence. Urgent precisely because it's embarrassing: if a reviewer reads "we reshape the curve region by region" and thinks "that's just undervolting," the causal design underneath never gets read charitably.

4. Draft the §5.7 pre-emption of the saturation objection. Kills the most obvious referee report in advance.

Off the list: dataset prep and the ML framing — exactly the kind of work that expands to fill however much time is left. Give the literature follow-ups a triggering date, not a good feeling.

Grade: strong B+/A−, publishable at a workshop once 1–4 land.

Response D — "is 'novel enough to publish' even a coherent gate here?"First Principles · Sonnet

Publication novelty gates exist to ration reviewer attention in a system where the default submitter is a funded lab with institutional incentive to overclaim. None of that describes this artifact. Applying the lab-grade gate asks "did a novel claim survive" when the actual production is "did a process survive" — and the process is the finding.

Every one of the seven retractions fired on the framing sentence, not the measurement. The CSVs never lied; the search discipline caught the overclaim every time before it calcified. That is a working falsification loop, self-applied, at a cadence most funded labs don't sustain because their incentives point the other way.

Reframe: stop optimising for "beat the literature", start optimising for "publish the method that keeps catching you", with the causal manipulation and the 2060S boundary as the empirical anchor.

Response E — "the upside nobody is naming is the tooling"Expansionist · Sonnet

282 claims that are rendered strings computed from raw data at check time, not stored expected values, asserted to appear verbatim-once in the prose. That is a general solution to a problem every empirical field has and almost nobody has open-sourced cleanly. Release it standalone with the Headroom corpus as its worked example and this stops being one undergrad's GPU paper.

Second: the 2060 Super boundary isn't a footnote, it's a second paper — an independent, citable correction to existing literature, and probably the fastest actual publication here.

Third: the Afterburner curve decode plus the crossbar finding is hardware reverse-engineering documentation the overclocking community wants independently of any academic outcome.

The biggest miss: treating this as one paper instead of three artifacts with different audiences and different time-to-publish.

Response A — "they're doing literature search backwards"Outsider · Haiku

Seven retractions isn't a sign they're being too bold; it's a sign the workflow is broken. They're writing up results, discovering during that process what's already published, and retracting. That's expensive and demoralizing.

The experimental work looks genuinely solid. But they're discovering prior art at the publishing stage instead of using it to scope the claim before experiments. The citation errors suggest they're working too fast or too tired at write-up. That's a symptom, not the disease.

Don't publish yet. Do a full literature review — actually read the field, not search-as-you-write. The alternative is retracting in print instead of in a private repo.

They built a model that lost and published it. That's exactly right. The experimental chops are there; the meta-process isn't.
