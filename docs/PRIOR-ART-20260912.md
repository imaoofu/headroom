# Prior art search — claims surviving the ridge-point retraction

Date: 2026-09-12. Trigger: the project's headline mechanism ("the efficiency optimum is the
highest frequency the applied V/F curve reaches at the card's load-floor voltage") was found to
be the published RIDGE POINT (van Werkhoven et al., arXiv 2211.07260) — the project's second
novelty retraction. This document searches the five claims that were said to survive that
retraction: causal manipulation (A), per-card floor voltage non-transfer (B), consumer silicon
(C), crossbar starvation (D), and the wrong-range argument against published consumer datasets
(E). Per the SEARCH RULE, this is a log of queries and what they found, not a conclusion to be
taken on faith — repeat and extend it before relying on it further.

**Coverage caveat, stated once and applying throughout:** this search used general web search and
direct fetch only. No IEEE Xplore, ACM DL, or Google Scholar citation-graph search was performed.
Several candidate PDFs returned corrupted, binary, or structural text to the fetch tool rather than
readable body text, so a number of citations below rest on search snippets or abstracts, not full
text — each is marked accordingly. "Not found" below means "not found by this search," never
"proven absent."

## A — Causal manipulation of the V/F curve

**Claim under test:** reshaping the V/F curve by hand, re-sweeping frequency, and showing the
efficiency optimum relocates as a consequence — with a negative control (perturbing a curve
region that should not matter, confirming it doesn't) and predictions registered before
collection.

**Verdict: appears novel.** No paper or documented study of any kind — academic, patent, or
enthusiast — was found that performs this experiment. Every comparator located falls into one of
two categories, neither of which is this:

1. **Passive observation of the vendor-shipped curve.** Van Werkhoven et al. (arXiv 2211.07260)
   define the ridge point by querying `nvidia-smi -q -d VOLTAGE` on the factory curve; they do not
   modify it. Afzal et al. (arXiv 2607.00819) fit a piecewise power model to the observed
   "transition frequency" on A40/A100/H100/H200, again without reshaping the curve.
2. **Fixed-frequency undervolting to find Vmin**, without a re-sweep to check whether an
   optimal *frequency* moves. Leng et al. (MICRO 2015) and Trakosa et al. (IOLTS 2025, "NAVIgator")
   both reduce voltage at one clock point per test. The closest single match, Mei et al.'s K20
   firmware study (arXiv 1407.8116), reprogrammed the GPU's voltage states but evaluated the result
   at a constant 800 MHz — never swept.

The enthusiast/reverse-engineering side confirms the *tooling* for this kind of manipulation is
known — `nvcurve` and the LACT project document per-point V/F curve editing via the same
undocumented NVAPI surface Afterburner uses — but none of that is a research study with an
efficiency-optimum-moves finding, a negative control, or preregistration.

Both adversarial refutation passes agreed: after trying mobile-SoC OPP-table vocabulary, patent
search terms ("voltage frequency table reshaping," "guardband relaxation"), causal/counterfactual
terminology, and "registered predictions," nothing surfaced that does the manipulation. This is
the project's strongest of the five claims.

**Citations:**
- van Werkhoven et al., arXiv 2211.07260 (2022), https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07260 — read full
  text. Confirms passive observation only; no curve reshaping, no negative control, no
  preregistration.
- Afzal et al., arXiv 2607.00819 (2026) — abstract only (companion full-text read on the "wrong
  range" claim below, but the manipulation question was assessed from the abstract's stated
  method: fitting a model to measured frequency/power points, not reshaping a curve).
- Leng et al., "Safe Limits on Voltage Reduction Efficiency in GPUs," MICRO 2015,
  https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2830772.2830811 — abstract only in this pass (full text read
  separately for claim B; see there). Fixed-frequency Vmin characterization, no frequency
  re-sweep.
- Mei et al. (K20 firmware study), arXiv 1407.8116, https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.8116 — search
  snippet only. Reprogrammed firmware voltage states, evaluated at one fixed 800 MHz clock.
- `nvcurve` / LACT per-point V/F curve editing, GitHub issue #936,
  https://github.com/ilya-zlobintsev/LACT/issues/936 — search snippet only. Confirms the
  manipulation is technically reachable via the same NVAPI surface; not a research finding.

**Not reached:** Leng MICRO'15's own PDF, the Afzal PDF, the Mei firmware PDF, and a Texas State
K20 DVFS paper all returned only structural/binary text to the fetch tool — none were read
verbatim. No IEEE Xplore/ACM DL/Google Scholar search was run, so a paywalled ISCA/HPCA/DAC/ISLPED
paper doing exactly this experiment under a name not tried (e.g. "V/F point remapping," "guardband
relaxation sweep," "P-state curve perturbation ablation") cannot be ruled out. No non-English
literature was searched.

## B — The load-floor voltage is per-card and does not transfer

**Claim under test:** the voltage at which the V/F curve's low-frequency plateau sits (0.631 /
0.720 / 0.756 / 0.812 V across this project's four chips) is a property of the individual chip,
not the model, and cannot be borrowed from one card to predict another's optimum.

**Verdict: prior art, at the general level.** The proposition that GPU minimum/floor voltage
varies chip-to-chip due to process variation, and cannot be borrowed between nominally identical
cards, is established — on consumer silicon, across two vendors, over a decade:

> "the Vmin on the GTX 780-2 card is consistently higher than the values on the GTX 780-5 card by
> a relatively constant offset, which increases slightly as the frequency increases"
> — Leng et al., MICRO 2015, on five physical consumer GTX 780 cards, attributed to process
> variation.

Trakosa et al. (IOLTS 2025, "NAVIgator") independently confirm the same on six consumer AMD Radeon
boards (RX 7600/7700/7800 XT, two boards per model) this year:

> "[we] can clearly see that the 7800A GPU has significantly lower Vmin than its B counterpart...
> The differences observed between cards of the same model for a given workload are attributed to
> process variation."

**What narrows but survives:** neither paper connects the per-chip voltage offset to a *shift in
an efficiency-optimal frequency* located via a full sweep. Both measure Vmin at a fixed frequency
(near boost, in NAVIgator's case) — not the top-of-the-flat-region quantity this project calls the
load floor, and not paired with a frequency sweep that shows where the optimum lands. The
project's defensible claim narrows from "the floor voltage is per-card" (not novel) to: *the
floor voltage's effect on where the efficiency optimum sits is per-card, and borrowing another
card's value mispredicts it by a measured, specific amount* (the 5060 Ti's 0.720 V vs. the 3060's
0.756 V predicting ~1530 MHz against a true 1260 MHz optimum — a 270 MHz error). No paper found
makes that connection.

**Citations:**
- Leng, Buyuktosunoglu, Bertran, Bose, Reddi, "Safe Limits on Voltage Reduction Efficiency in
  GPUs," MICRO-48, 2015, https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2830772.2830811 (mirror:
  https://cs.sjtu.edu.cn/~leng-jw/resources/Files/leng15micro-gpuvminexp.pdf) — read full text (via
  the mirror in the second search pass; the PDF returned only structural/binary data on a later
  attempt, so treat the full-text read as achieved once, not reliably repeatable). Consumer GTX
  480/580/680/780, five physical 780 units, per-card Vmin offset ~0.07 V, attributed to process
  variation. Measured at fixed frequency, no ridge/optimum sweep.
- Trakosa, Chatzopoulos, Papadimitriou, Gizopoulos, "NAVIgator: Exploring the Voltage Limits of
  AMD NAVI GPUs for Energy Efficient Computing," IOLTS 2025,
  https://www.ceid.upatras.gr/webpages/faculty/gpapad/assets/papers/iolts2025_trakosa.pdf — read
  full text. Six consumer Radeon boards, explicit board-to-board Vmin variation attributed to
  process variation. Fixed frequency near boost, no ridge/optimum connection.
- NVIDIA Speedo / Continuous Virtual Binning (CVB) telemetry, as described by Igor'sLab,

  — search snippet only. States the factory V/F curve's voltage-per-frequency-point is

## ✅ UPDATE 2026-09-12 — the blocked HotPower 2013 paper was READ, by Raymond, not by this search

This document named it "the single most-promising remaining candidate ... unresolved either way".
It is now resolved. **Raymond obtained and read the paper; this section is written from his reading
and from figures he supplied, not from a fetch.** Everything below is therefore second-hand to this
document but first-hand to the project — a better provenance than the snippets it replaces.

🔑 **AND IT CORRECTS THIS DOCUMENT'S OWN SNIPPET-DERIVED CITATION.** The entry below said
"GTX 480, 480–1080 MHz sweep". The paper actually fixes core voltage at **1.049 V and 0.849 V** and
sweeps **fcore from 480 to 880 MHz**, on **consumer Fermi/Kepler-era GeForce parts** (Raymond reads
the card list as GTX 280 / 380 / 560 Ti — ⚠️ *"GTX 380" was never a retail product, so the middle
card needs pinning down before citation*). A snippet got the range and the hardware wrong in a
citation this document marked `search snippet only`. **The marking worked; the citation did not.**

### What the paper establishes

| | |
|---|---|
| Scope | **37 benchmark applications**, consumer GeForce hardware |
| Control | **Vcore AND fcore both scaled directly** — real voltage writes, which this era of card allowed |
| Sweep | fcore 480→880 MHz at two fixed core voltages; core-voltage offsets **−200 mV to +50 mV** |
| Figure 3 | **Vcore against f\*core**, the maximum *stable* core frequency: ~880 MHz at 0.85 V rising to ~1000 MHz at 1.1 V |
| Figure 4 | Average energy savings **~19% at −200 mV**, falling monotonically and going **negative at +50 mV** |
| Figure 5 | A per-program "best voltage", clustered at **~0.85 V for nearly every one of the 37** |

Their conclusion, verbatim: *"scaling down the GPU core voltage can significantly save the energy
when working at appropriate core frequency"*, and *"it is not a trivial task to find the optimal
setting of GPU DVFS"* — with a statistical power model named as **future work**.

### What it does to each claim

**C — confirmed PRIOR ART, and more strongly than before.** This is not a frequency-only sweep; it
is genuine voltage scaling on consumer silicon in 2013, across 37 applications. Any sentence in the
paper implying consumer DVFS energy measurement is unexplored is wrong and must go.

**E — reinforces the broad retraction.** Below-stock consumer measurement with voltage control has
existed since 2013.

**B — unresolved, and this paper may bear on it.** If its per-card f\*core frontiers differ between
the three cards, that is further prior art for per-chip voltage behaviour. ⚠️ Not visible in the
figures supplied; needs checking in the text.

**A — NOT pre-empted, on this evidence, but closer than anything else found.** Three distinctions,
all checkable rather than rhetorical:

1. 🔑 **f\*core is a STABILITY frontier, not a curve floor.** They lower voltage and find the
   highest clock that still runs. This project reads where the *vendor's shipped curve* stops
   lowering voltage. Related quantities, different constructs — the same distinction that separates
   Leng's Vmin from the load floor.
2. **Their "best voltage" sits at 0.85 V for nearly all 37 programs — the bottom edge of the tested
   range.** That is a range limit, not an interior optimum, so the paper does not demonstrate an
   optimum *moving* to a located position.
3. **No negative control, and no regional manipulation.** They shift voltage globally; this project
   changes one region of the curve and holds another fixed, and separately changes the other region
   and shows nothing moves.

⚠️ **The decisive question this document cannot answer:** does the paper anywhere report the
energy-optimal FREQUENCY shifting as a consequence of the voltage change — as opposed to reporting
energy savings at each setting? If it does, claim A narrows sharply. Figure 3 is the stability
frontier and Figure 4 is savings-versus-offset; neither is that plot. **Until someone checks the
body text, treat A as open rather than confirmed.**

### 🔑 The methodological point worth keeping

**They had MORE control than this project does.** Core voltage was directly writable on that
hardware. On current consumer NVIDIA parts it is not — zero voltage exports across all 260 NVML
device functions, confirmed by direct probing — which is why this project reshapes the curve by
hand in Afterburner and reads voltage through HWiNFO instead.

So the honest framing is not "nobody measured this on consumer parts". It is **"the method that
produced those 2013 results is no longer available on current consumer hardware, and what replaced
it measures something adjacent rather than the same thing"**. That is a narrower and more
defensible reason for redoing the work than novelty.

## C — Efficiency optimum located on consumer GeForce/Radeon silicon

**Claim under test:** no published study has swept frequency broadly enough to locate a
ridge-point-style efficiency optimum on consumer (GeForce/Radeon) hardware; prior ridge-point work
used datacenter (A100) and workstation (RTX A4000) parts only.

**Verdict: prior art, as broadly stated.** This is false at the general level. Two lines of work,
a decade apart, swept frequency on consumer GeForce silicon and located an efficiency optimum:

- Mei et al. (HotPower 2013) swept a **GTX 480** (Fermi, consumer, ~700 MHz stock) across
  480–1080 MHz combined with multiple voltage levels — spanning below AND above stock — reporting
  ~19.28% average energy reduction at an "appropriate" (often lower) frequency/voltage, at ≤4%
  performance cost.
- Tang et al. (arXiv 1905.11012, e-Energy 2019) swept a **GTX 2080 Ti** (Turing, consumer) from
  950–1950 MHz against a 1350 MHz default — down to 70% of stock — and reported energy-optimal
  frequencies with 8.7–26.4% savings depending on training/inference. This is a more recent
  consumer architecture than either of the project's own two cited comparison datasets (1080 Ti,
  2070 Super).
- Mei et al.'s extended survey (arXiv 1610.01784) adds a GTX 680 (Kepler, consumer), reporting
  ~15.8–21% voltage-reduction savings and noting some applications' optimal frequency falls below
  default.

**What narrows but survives:** none of these connect the optimum to a *load-floor voltage
mechanism* in the ridge-point sense, none manipulate the V/F curve causally, and none test four
chips across three recent architectures (Blackwell/Ampere/Turing) with a negative control. The
project cannot claim "nobody has located an efficiency optimum on consumer parts" — that is done —
but can still claim nobody has done so via this project's specific causal design, on this
generation of hardware, with this cross-architecture replication.

**Citations:**
- Mei, Yung, Zhao, Chu, "A Measurement Study of GPU DVFS on Energy Conservation," HotPower 2013,
  https://www.comp.hkbu.edu.hk/~chxw/papers/hotpower_2013.pdf — search snippet only (certificate
  errors on direct fetch). GTX 480, 480–1080 MHz sweep, ~19% savings below default.
- Tang, Chu et al., "The Impact of GPU DVFS on the Energy and Performance of Deep Learning,"
  e-Energy 2019, arXiv 1905.11012, https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11012 — **read full text**. GTX 2080
  Ti swept 950–1950 MHz vs. 1350 MHz stock; energy-optimal points found below stock.
- Mei et al., "A Survey and Measurement Study of GPU DVFS on Energy Conservation," arXiv 1610.01784,
  https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.01784 — search snippet only. GTX 480/680, 15.8–21% savings.
- van Werkhoven et al., arXiv 2211.07260 — read full text (as above). Confirms A100/A4000 only,
  zero consumer parts — the gap the project is trying to fill, and the one point where the
  project's framing of C is accurate.

**Not reached:** the HotPower 2013 PDF and its ScienceDirect-hosted extension both returned only
snippets (403/certificate errors both attempts) — whether they explicitly frame their finding as a
ridge-point/load-floor mechanism, or release raw open data, is unconfirmed. Whether Mei et al. or
Tang et al. released their raw sweep data as an open, downloadable dataset (as opposed to only
aggregate paper results) was not confirmed either way — this matters for how much of the *open
dataset* framing (as opposed to the *measurement exists at all* framing) survives. No IEEE
Xplore/ACM DL/Google Scholar search. No non-English literature.

## D — Crossbar starvation (flattened core curve → pinned XBAR clock → memory bandwidth plateau)

**Claim under test:** flattening the core V/F curve pins core voltage, which pins the crossbar
(XBAR/interconnect) clock near a fixed value instead of letting it track the core, which collapses
memory bandwidth to a plateau while DRAM clock is unaffected — measured via HWiNFO's crossbar
sensor, invisible to nvidia-smi/NVML.

**Verdict: partially novel.** The general substrate fact is prior art, independently discovered
close to the same time as this project's own finding, but not on the same chip and not via the
same causal chain:

> "XBAR is rarely shown at all" in public NVIDIA overclocking interfaces, and setting a memory
> clock ceiling "propagates through shared voltage rails to suppress the actual XBAR clock" —
> loong0x00, "XBAR in NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs: A Physical Clock Domain Ignored by Public Tooling,"
> published 2026-08-13, on an **RTX 5090** (GB202).

The companion LACT GitHub issue #1147 (opened 2026-08-10, ASUS ROG Astral RTX 5090) independently
confirms runtime-adjustable XBAR clock and a *separate* per-domain voltage offset (MSVDD), with
measured throughput effects from each. Both predate or closely coincide with this project's own
`membw`-plateau discovery (2026-08-19 → 08-21) by one to two weeks, on the same Blackwell
generation but a different chip (GB202/RTX 5090 vs. this project's GB206/RTX 5060 Ti), reached via
Linux register-level reverse engineering rather than HWiNFO+Afterburner.

**What is not found anywhere in this search, academic or community:** a memory-bandwidth-plateau
measurement; a quantified crossbar/core clock-ratio collapse (this project's 0.928–0.976 →
0.942→0.726); or a stock-vs-tuned control isolating the effect. The adversarial pass specifically
checked and ruled out four adjacent candidates: an Intel graphics-sub-domain-voltage patent family
(covers per-EU/sampler voltage, not bandwidth); the MICRO'24 "Uncovering Real GPU NoC
Characteristics" paper (NoC latency/bandwidth non-uniformity, not voltage-driven crossbar
pinning); an HBM voltage-underscaling paper (arXiv 2101.00969) that explicitly finds undervolting
HBM does *not* affect bandwidth — the opposite mechanism; and Guerreiro's multi-domain GPU power
models (whose "memory domain" means DRAM/L2/shared-memory power, not an interconnect clock
domain). CPU-side Uncore Frequency Scaling literature (Intel UFS: arXiv 2502.03796; the GAMESS
core/uncore comparison paper; "Uncore Encore," ACM CCS 2023) establishes the general pattern of an
independent interconnect clock domain gating bandwidth is old and well-known — but CPU-only, not
voltage-driven, not GPU.

**Recommendation:** cite the loong0x00/LACT material as prior art for "XBAR is a real,
voltage-coupled, publicly-hidden clock domain on Blackwell" — do not present that half as this
project's discovery. The bandwidth-plateau mechanism and its quantified crossbar/core ratio
collapse remain the project's own, as far as this search reached.

**Citations:**
- loong0x00, "XBAR in NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs: A Physical Clock Domain Ignored by Public Tooling,"
  2026-08-13, https://loong0x00.com/notes/blackwell-xbar-physical-clock-domain/ — read full text
  (via fetch). RTX 5090/GB202. No bandwidth-plateau or ratio-collapse finding.
- ilya-zlobintsev/LACT, GitHub issue #1147, opened 2026-08-10,
  https://github.com/ilya-zlobintsev/LACT/issues/1147 — read full text (via fetch). RTX 5090,
  independent XBAR clock and MSVDD voltage control confirmed; no bandwidth-plateau finding.
- "Exploring Uncore Frequency Scaling for Heterogeneous Computing," arXiv 2502.03796,
  https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03796 — search snippet only. CPU-side analogue, general precedent
  only.
- "Comparisons of core and uncore frequency scaling modes in quantum chemistry application
  GAMESS," https://www.researchgate.net/publication/322381268 — search snippet only. CPU-side,
  same general precedent.
- "Uncore Encore: Covert Channels Exploiting Uncore Frequency Scaling," ACM CCS workshop 2023,
  https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3613424.3614259 — search snippet only. CPU-side,
  background only.
- HBM voltage-underscaling paper, arXiv 2101.00969 — not directly cited with title/authors
  captured in the source material provided; noted only as ruled out in the adversarial pass

**Not reached:** the MICRO'24 NoC-characterization paper, Guerreiro's HPCA'18 paper and PhD
dissertation, the Intel sub-domain-voltage patent, and a "DUB NoC" paper all returned corrupted or

bandwidth-plateau finding is added there after 2026-09-12.

## E — Published consumer DVFS datasets sweep the wrong range

**Claim under test:** two narrower sub-claims bundled together — (i) the specific open,
downloadable CSVs cited (GTX 1080 Ti, RTX 2070 Super) sweep only 95–126% / 95–118% of rated boost,
i.e. overclocking only; and (ii), built on top of (i), "nobody has published a below-stock
efficiency optimum on consumer silicon."

**Verdict: prior art on the broad claim (ii); partially novel on the narrow claim (i).** Sub-claim
(ii) is directly contradicted, twice, a decade apart, by the same sources cited under claim C:

- Mei et al. (HotPower 2013) swept a consumer GTX 480 down to 480 MHz against a ~700 MHz stock
  clock and reported energy savings from running *below* default.
- Tang et al. (arXiv 1905.11012) swept a consumer RTX 2080 Ti down to 950 MHz against a 1350 MHz
  default (70% of stock) and reported an energy-optimal frequency found via that below-stock
  sweep.

"Nobody has published this" is false. What was not found anywhere in this search is the narrower
sub-claim (i): a stated critique that these two *specific* open CSV datasets happen to sweep only
the overclocking range. No prior source makes that meta-observation, and this search could not
even locate the original paper or repository behind either CSV to check it independently.

**Recommendation:** the paper should soften from "nobody has published this" to something closer
to: academic below-stock consumer sweeps exist and go back to 2013, but the two open,
downloadable, redistributable datasets this project found happen to be overclocking-only — the
gap is in open reusable data at this specific range, not in the measurement ever having been made.
This is a materially weaker and more defensible claim than the current CLAUDE.md framing.

**Citations:**
- Mei et al., HotPower 2013 (as above) — search snippet only.
- Tang et al., arXiv 1905.11012 (as above) — **read full text**.
- Mei et al., arXiv 1610.01784 (as above) — search snippet only.
- Afzal et al., arXiv 2607.00819, https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00819v1 — **read full text**. Modern
  (2026) full-range, below-stock sweeps (A40 0.21–1.74 GHz, A100 0.21–1.41 GHz, H100 0.345–1.98
  GHz, H200 0.40–1.98 GHz) with a released dataset — but datacenter/professional parts only, not
  consumer. Shows the "full range, below stock, open dataset" combination is being done right now,
  elsewhere, just not yet for consumer cards — narrows how novel the *gap* itself can be claimed to
  be.
- Leng et al., MICRO 2015 — listed here only as already-known adjacent guardband literature (the
  project's own 2026-08-14 retraction already covers this).

**Not reached:** the original source/host for the project's own two cited consumer datasets (the
1080 Ti and 2070 Super CSVs) could not be located despite multiple targeted searches, so their
95–126%/95–118%-of-boost characterization is taken as given from CLAUDE.md, not independently

## What this means for the paper

**Sections needing rewriting, concretely:**

1. Wherever the paper currently states or implies "no published study has located an efficiency
   optimum on consumer GPUs" (this maps to the §2.x related-work framing feeding the load-floor
   sections, per CLAUDE.md's 2026-09-12 entry that "Sections 2.1–2.5 need rewriting"), that
   sentence is now **false as stated** and must be narrowed. Cite Mei et al. (2013) and Tang et al.
   (2019) as prior consumer measurements, and reframe the contribution as the causal design (A) on
   modern consumer parts (C), not as first measurement.
2. The "wrong range" argument (§ wherever `analysis/compare_consumer.py`'s output is written up,
   likely near 5.5–5.6 per the mechanism sections) should be narrowed from "consumer data doesn't
   exist below stock" to "the two specific open datasets we found don't cover below stock" —
   the broader energy-optimum-exists-below-stock finding is not new; the absence of an *open,
   redistributable* dataset at that range plausibly still is, but was not confirmed either.
3. The floor-voltage-per-card claim (§5.5.x, wherever 0.631/0.720/0.756/0.812 V is presented)
   needs a citation to Leng et al. (2015) and Trakosa et al. (2025) as prior art for "floor/Vmin
   voltage is per-chip" in general, with the paper's own contribution narrowed to the connection
   between that per-chip voltage and a *shifted optimal frequency*, quantified as a specific
   misprediction magnitude (the 270 MHz error from borrowing 0.720 V for the 3060). Do not present
   "per-card voltage" alone as a finding.
4. §D's crossbar-starvation section needs a citation to the loong0x00/LACT material as
   corroborating (not competing) prior art that XBAR is a real, hidden, voltage-linked domain on
   Blackwell — found independently, one to two weeks before this project's own result, on a
   different chip. The bandwidth-plateau mechanism itself appears to remain the project's own.
5. Any lineage/related-work section discussing the ridge point's own history (per the
   already-completed dimension F search) should note the term "ridge point" is borrowed from the
   2009 Roofline model (a different axis entirely — arithmetic intensity, not frequency) so the
   name's age is not mistaken for the frequency-domain concept's age.

**Contribution statement, revised:** not "we measured an efficiency optimum on consumer GPUs" —
that exists since 2013. Not "the floor voltage is per-chip" — that exists since 2015. The
defensible claim is narrower and should say so plainly: *this project causally manipulated a
consumer GPU's V/F curve, with a negative control and pre-registered predictions, and showed the
efficiency-optimal frequency moves as a direct consequence — replicated across four consumer chips
and three architectures, with a measured, non-transferable floor voltage per chip, and a novel
crossbar-starvation mechanism explaining why an undervolt that helps compute-bound workloads
actively harms bandwidth-bound ones.* That is smaller than what CLAUDE.md currently claims survived
the 09-12 retraction, and it should be stated at that size, not larger.
